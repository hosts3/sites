Why This Comparison Matters More in Miami Than Almost Anywhere Else

Miami-Dade is one of the most competitive insurance markets in the United States. Walk into any strip mall from Little Havana to Kendall and you'll find storefronts for State Farm, Allstate, Primerica, and a dozen independent agencies — all quoting "the same" life and health products at very different levels of usefulness.

The confusion is understandable. Both types of agents are licensed by the Florida Department of Financial Services. Both are paid by the insurance company, not by you. Both will tell you the consultation is free. So where does the difference in cost actually show up? It shows up in four places:

  1. Option cost — how many carriers can be shopped for your specific health history or budget.
  2. Time cost — how many appointments and applications you sit through before you find a workable rate.
  3. Renewal cost — what happens in year two, three, and ten when rates change or a plan leaves your county.
  4. Denial cost — what happens when a carrier declines you and your agent has nowhere else to send the case.

That's the framework we'll use below. Not "who is nicer," but which structure costs you less over the life of the policy.

Deep Dive: How Each Model Actually Costs You Money

1. Independent Agents: The Real Commission Math

An independent agent — sometimes called a broker — holds appointments with multiple insurance carriers. At Biscayne Bay Life & Health Insurance Group in Little Havana, for example, that means life appointments with Mutual of Omaha, Foresters Financial, Americo, John Hancock, Transamerica, North American, Nationwide, and Gerber Life, plus health and Medicare contracts with Florida Blue, UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Aetna, Cigna, Ambetter, Molina Healthcare, Oscar Health, Doctor's Health Plan, and Simply Healthcare.

Here is what almost no one explains to consumers: your premium does not change based on which agent writes the policy. Life insurance rates are filed with the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation by age, gender, health class, and term length. Medicare Advantage and Part D commissions are set annually by CMS and are fixed — a Medicare agent cannot legally charge you more or less on a $0-premium Advantage plan. Medigap commissions are a regulated percentage of the filed premium.

So an independent agent typically earns:

  • Term life: a first-year commission that's a percentage of the first-year premium, then a much smaller renewal percentage in years two and beyond. That structure is why a good independent agent has a financial reason to keep your policy on the books — which means keeping it competitive.
  • Medicare Advantage: a flat, CMS-set per-enrollment amount that is the same regardless of which plan you choose. There is no financial upside to steering you toward a specific carrier.
  • Medicare Supplement (Medigap): a percentage of premium, which — because Plan G benefits are identical across carriers — creates an incentive to find you the lowest premium, not the highest.
  • ACA Marketplace: a carrier-set percentage of the premium. Since most Miami-Dade households qualify for premium tax credits, that commission is usually calculated after subsidies.

Concrete Miami pricing you can sanity-check against: a healthy 30-year-old can often get $250,000 of 20-year term coverage for roughly $18–$24 per month. A healthy 45-year-old can typically get $500,000 of 20-year term for about $58–$75 per month. Final expense coverage for ages 45–85 generally runs $32–$95 per month depending on age and whether you're buying $5,000 or $25,000 of coverage. Medigap Plan G in Miami-Dade typically sits between $115 and $210 per month depending on age and carrier, and many Medicare Advantage plans are available at a $0 monthly premium. On the ACA side, most Miami-Dade households receive premium tax credits, and a large share end up paying under $50 a month.

2. Captive Agents: Where the Hidden Cost Lives

A captive agent works for one carrier. They may be a W-2 employee or a contracted producer, and they are generally restricted to that company's products — sometimes with a small "brokerage arm" they're allowed to use only after the captive product is declined.

The premium you pay a captive agent is also the same filed rate. So where's the cost? It's the opportunity cost of a single shelf. Three examples that come up constantly in Miami:

  1. Underwriting mismatch. One carrier may treat well-controlled type 2 diabetes, sleep apnea, or a history of anxiety differently from another. A captive agent has exactly one set of underwriting guidelines. If your profile lands you in a "Table 4" rate with their carrier, you pay that rate for 20 years — even though a competitor might have issued you Preferred.
  2. Medicare plan-year changes. Formularies, networks, and $0-premium availability shift every year in Miami-Dade. A captive agent can only offer their own plan. An independent agent can compare every contract in the county during Annual Enrollment (October 15 – December 7).
  3. ACA subsidies. Many captive-branded agencies don't write Marketplace plans at all. Households in Hialeah, Doral, and Homestead that qualify for large premium tax credits may never learn their net premium could be near zero.

There's also a structural pressure worth naming honestly: captive producers typically carry production quotas and "persistency" targets. That doesn't make them dishonest — but it does mean the recommendation you receive is filtered through what one company needs that quarter.

3. The "Buy Direct Online" Third Option (and What It Really Costs)

Direct-to-consumer websites are not automatically cheaper. For term life, some carriers do file a slightly lower no-commission rate band, but the difference is typically small and comes with real trade-offs: no help with the application, no assistance if the underwriter asks for medical records, no claims advocacy for your beneficiary, and no one to call when a rate increases in year eight. For Medicare and ACA plans, buying "direct" usually just routes you back to a licensed agent anyway — the carrier's own call center.

4. Nine Cost Questions to Ask Any Agent in Miami

Print this list. Ask it before you sign anything.

  1. How many carriers are you actively appointed with for my product type?
  2. Are you captive to one company, or independent?
  3. How exactly are you compensated — and does it differ between the options you're showing me?
  4. If I'm declined, what's your Plan B carrier?
  5. What happens to my premium at renewal, and can we re-shop it?
  6. Do you handle claims paperwork for my beneficiary?
  7. Is there any fee I pay you directly? (In Florida, the honest answer for life and health is no.)
  8. Will you review my policy annually at no charge?
  9. Can you show me the same coverage at three different carriers side by side?

If an agent can't answer questions 1, 4, and 6 confidently, you are likely talking to a captive producer — which is fine, as long as you understand the limitation going in.

The Verdict: Which Agent Type Costs You Less?

For life and health insurance buyers in Miami, an independent agent almost always costs less — because the cost isn't the premium, it's the options you forfeit. Since your monthly rate is filed with the state and identical across channels, choosing a single-carrier agent means accepting whatever one underwriter decides about your health history, your county's plan network, and your renewal increases.

That said, a captive agent is a reasonable choice in specific scenarios. Here's the honest breakdown:

Choose an INDEPENDENT agent if…

  • You're a 1099 worker, gig driver, or restaurant/hospitality employee with no employer benefits
  • You have any health history — diabetes, sleep apnea, high blood pressure, past surgeries
  • You're turning 65 or reviewing Medicare Advantage during Annual Enrollment
  • You need ACA Marketplace subsidies and want to compare Ambetter vs. Molina vs. Oscar vs. Florida Blue
  • You want one agent to handle life, Medicare, and group benefits for your small business
  • You're bilingual and want service in Spanish or Haitian Creole

A CAPTIVE agent may be fine if…

  • You already bank with that brand and value the single-app experience
  • You're in excellent health and the captive carrier's rate is genuinely competitive
  • You need only one simple product, such as a basic term policy
  • You've compared the captive quote against at least two independent quotes

The rule of thumb: never sign a life or health application until you've seen at least three carrier illustrations side by side. That takes one 30-minute appointment with an independent agent and costs you nothing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do independent agents charge more than captive agents?

No. In Florida, life insurance premiums are filed with the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation, and Medicare commissions are set by CMS. A policy purchased through an independent agent in Miami costs exactly the same as the identical policy purchased through a captive agent or the carrier's own website. Agents are compensated by the insurance company, and consultations are free to you.

Why do some quotes come back different if rates are filed?

Because you're rarely comparing identical policies. Differences usually come from the health class you're approved at, the term length, whether the policy is convertible, the waiver-of-premium rider, the carrier's separate rate band for tobacco use, and — for health plans — the network and formulary. Two quotes that look like the same coverage often aren't.

Is a captive agent ever cheaper for Medicare?

For Medicare Advantage, no — the monthly premium and CMS-set commission are fixed, so a captive agent cannot pass savings to you. For Medigap, the differences come from carrier pricing, age-rating methods, and household discounts, which is precisely why comparing multiple carriers matters. Plan G rates in Miami-Dade typically range from about $115 to $210 per month depending on age and carrier for identical benefits.

What happens if I'm declined by a captive agent's carrier?

With a captive agent, the process usually ends there — you have to start over with a new application elsewhere. With an independent agent, the case is immediately re-shopped to a carrier with more lenient underwriting, or to a simplified-issue or guaranteed-issue product if you're between ages 50 and 85.

Can I switch from a captive agent to an independent agent mid-policy?

Yes, in most cases. Your existing policy stays with its carrier — an independent agent doesn't "take over" the contract, but they can review it, tell you whether a better-priced equivalent exists, and handle a new application if a replacement genuinely benefits you. Always compare guaranteed values and surrender charges on permanent policies before replacing anything.

Are agents paid differently depending on the plan they sell me?

For Medicare Advantage, no — CMS standardizes it. For life and ACA policies, carriers pay different percentages, which is exactly why we disclose our compensation and why we show you multiple carriers side by side instead of one recommendation. If a client finds a lower rate for identical coverage anywhere, we'll match it or explain the difference in writing.

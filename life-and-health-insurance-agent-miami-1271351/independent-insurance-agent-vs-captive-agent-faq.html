Why This Comparison Matters More in Miami Than Almost Anywhere Else

Miami-Dade is one of the most competitive insurance markets in the United States. Within a 20-mile radius of Little Havana you can find more than 40 Medicare Advantage plans, a dozen ACA Marketplace carriers, and dozens of life insurance companies writing term, final expense, and indexed universal life policies. That abundance is a gift — and a trap. The person who helps you enroll decides which slice of that market you ever get to see.

That is the entire point of the independent insurance agent vs captive agent FAQ Life and Health Insurance agent conversation. It is not about who is more honest or more experienced. It is about how many carriers an agent can legally quote, and what happens to your policy when the carrier does something you don't like.

This guide answers the questions we hear every week at our Little Havana and Kendall offices — in plain English, with real numbers, so you can choose the right type of agent for your situation.

Key takeaways

  • A captive agent works for one carrier. An independent agent is appointed with many carriers.
  • You do not pay more when you use an independent agent — commissions are paid by the carrier, not by you.
  • In Florida, the license is the same (2-15 or 2-40). What differs is the contract and the carrier appointments.
  • For Medicare and ACA plans, carrier choice and drug/formulary coverage often matter more than the agent's brand logo.
  • For life insurance, carrier choice drives underwriting outcomes — especially with health conditions.

Deep Dive: How Each Type of Agent Actually Works

1. What a captive agent is

A captive agent holds a contract with a single insurance company. State Farm, New York Life, and many bank-channel agents are the classic examples. The carrier trains them, provides office support, and pays a salary or commission tied to that one product line. There is nothing shady about this arrangement — it's how a huge share of America buys insurance.

The limitation is structural, not personal. If you walk into a captive life agent's office with a controlled A1C and a history of sleep apnea, that agent can only submit you to one underwriter. If that underwriter declines you or offers Table 4 rates, your only option is to start over somewhere else. An independent agent in the same situation submits to four carriers the same afternoon and brings back the best offer.

2. What an independent agent is

An independent agent is appointed with multiple carriers. In Florida, the license itself is identical — a 2-15 Life, Health & Annuity license or a 2-40 Health license issued by the Florida Department of Financial Services. What differs is the appointment paperwork: independent agents carry appointments with many insurers at once.

At our agency, that means an ACA client can compare Ambetter, Florida Blue, Molina, Oscar, Cigna and Simply Healthcare in one sitting, and a Medicare client can run a drug list through every Part D formulary available in Miami-Dade before choosing. A life insurance client can be shopped to Mutual of Omaha, Foresters, Americo, Transamerica, John Hancock, North American and Gerber in a single appointment.

3. The commission question — does independent cost more?

No. This is the single biggest myth in the independent insurance agent vs captive agent FAQ Life and Health Insurance agent debate. Life and health insurance premiums are filed with the state. The carrier sets the rate; the agent's commission comes out of that rate, not on top of it. Whether you buy a $250,000 20-year term policy through a captive agent or an independent one, the premium on the same policy from the same carrier is identical.

Where independent representation pays off is rate shopping. Sample Miami rates: a healthy 30-year-old can get $250,000 of 20-year term coverage from roughly $18–$24 per month, and a healthy 45-year-old can get $500,000 of 20-year term from roughly $58–$75 per month. Those spreads exist between carriers — an agent who only quotes one carrier can't show you the difference.

4. Medicare and ACA: where the difference is biggest

Medicare Advantage and Part D plans vary dramatically by county. A plan that covers your cardiologist may have a $47 copay for Tier 3 drugs, while another covers the same doctor with a $0 copay but excludes your inhaler. Only an agent who represents multiple carriers can run that comparison honestly.

The same is true of ACA Marketplace subsidies. Most Miami-Dade households qualify for premium tax credits, and many pay under $50 per month after subsidies are applied. A captive carrier agent can only show you their own on-exchange plan — not whether a competitor's silver plan gives you a better deductible-to-subsidy combination.

5. Service after the sale

This is the most underrated difference. Insurance is a 10-, 20-, or 30-year relationship. When a claim is filed, when a beneficiary needs to be updated after a divorce, when a carrier changes its drug formulary in the middle of the plan year — someone has to make the phone calls.

Independent agencies typically live on renewals and referrals, which means their economic incentive is to keep you happy for decades. That's why we guarantee a 24-hour response on every client question and handle carrier paperwork directly for beneficiaries filing life insurance claims.

6. When a captive agent is genuinely the better choice

We'll say it plainly: captive agents are sometimes the right answer. If you're a W-2 employee enrolling in your employer's group plan, the HR benefits team is effectively your captive channel and it's often the cheapest option available. If a specific carrier's brand promise — a mutual company's dividend history, for instance — is the thing you value most, working with that carrier directly is reasonable.

The problem only arises when you assume a captive agent has shown you "the market" when they've shown you one company's shelf.

The Verdict: Which Type of Agent Should You Use?

For most people in Miami-Dade buying individual life, health, Medicare, or final expense coverage, an independent agent produces a better outcome — as long as they are transparent about which carriers they represent and why they're recommending one over another.

The reasoning is straightforward: you get the same filed rates, the same carrier contracts, and the same legal protections, but you gain access to comparison shopping and an advocate who isn't contractually limited to defending one company's product line.

Choose an independent agent if you are:

  • Self-employed, a gig driver, or a 1099 contractor with no employer benefits
  • Turning 65 and comparing Medicare Advantage vs Medicare Supplement Plan G (typically $115–$210/month in Miami-Dade by age)
  • A family comparing ACA Marketplace plans and subsidy eligibility
  • Managing a health condition like diabetes, sleep apnea, or high blood pressure and needing flexible underwriting
  • Buying final expense coverage at ages 45–85 and wanting no-exam options

Choose a captive agent or your employer's group channel if you are:

  • Enrolling in employer-sponsored group life or health benefits
  • Deeply brand-loyal to one specific insurer and want that carrier's own staff
  • Handling a policy already issued by that carrier and needing direct servicing

One practical middle ground: ask any agent, "How many carriers can you quote for me today, and which ones?" A captive agent will name one. A good independent agent will name ten and explain why they'd start with three.

Independent Insurance Agent vs Captive Agent FAQ

Do I pay more when I use an independent insurance agent vs a captive agent?

No. In the independent insurance agent vs captive agent FAQ Life and Health Insurance agent discussion, this is the most common misperception. Life and health premiums are filed with the state and are identical regardless of which agent submits the application. Commissions are paid by the insurance carrier out of that filed premium. Your consultation, quote, and enrollment help cost you $0.

Is the licensing different for independent vs captive agents in Florida?

No. Every agent selling life and health insurance in Florida holds the same state license — a 2-15 (Life, Health & Annuity) or 2-40 (Health) license issued by the Florida Department of Financial Services, requiring pre-licensing education, a background check, and continuing education. The difference is the carrier appointments an agent holds, not the license itself.

Can an independent agent really save me money on life insurance?

They can save you money by finding a lower-priced carrier, not by discounting your premium. Example: a healthy 45-year-old in Miami shopping $500,000 of 20-year term coverage will see quotes ranging roughly from $58 to $75 per month depending on the company. The spread comes from different underwriting classes and carrier pricing, not from the agent.

Which is better for Medicare — captive or independent?

Independent is almost always better for Medicare, because Medicare Advantage and Part D coverage is county-specific and plan formularies differ. An agent appointed with Florida Blue, UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Aetna, Cigna, Simply Healthcare and others can check whether your doctors and prescriptions are covered under each plan before you enroll. A captive agent can only present their own company's plan.

What happens to my policy if I switch from a captive to an independent agent?

Your existing policy stays exactly as it is — same carrier, same premium, same contract. You can keep the policy and simply work with a different agent for future service, reviews, or new coverage. For Medicare Advantage and ACA plans, moving agents does not require re-enrolling; your plan continues uninterrupted.

Are independent agents less regulated or less trustworthy?

The opposite. Independent agents carry a Florida license and are appointed and audited by every carrier they represent, plus they must maintain continuing education and — for Medicare — annual AHIP certification and CMS compliance training. There is no regulatory difference in consumer protection between an independent and a captive agent.

How do I know an independent agent isn't just pushing the carrier that pays the most?

Ask two questions: "Which carriers did you consider and reject for me, and why?" and "What's your renewal-based service promise?" A credible agency will show you a side-by-side comparison and explain the trade-offs in writing. Commissions across major life carriers are broadly similar, so the incentive to push a bad fit is minimal compared to the incentive to keep a client for 20 years.
